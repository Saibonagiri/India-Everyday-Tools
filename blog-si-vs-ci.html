Simple Interest vs Compound Interest: What's the Difference

Two different ways interest grows — and why the difference matters more than it first appears.

Simple and compound interest are two of the most fundamental ideas in personal finance, and understanding the difference between them genuinely changes how you evaluate loans, deposits, and investments. They sound similar, but the gap between them widens significantly the longer money sits.

Simple interest: a straight line

Simple interest is calculated only on your original principal amount, for the entire time period, regardless of how much interest has already accumulated. The formula is: interest equals principal multiplied by rate multiplied by time, divided by 100. Because it's always calculated on the same original amount, simple interest grows in a straight, predictable line — the amount you earn (or owe) in year one is identical to what you'd earn in year five, assuming the rate stays constant.

Compound interest: growth on growth

Compound interest works differently — interest is calculated not just on your original principal, but also on any interest that's already been added to it. Each time interest compounds, it becomes part of the new base for calculating the next round of interest. This is why compound interest grows faster the longer it runs: you're effectively earning interest on your interest, not just on your original amount.

Why compounding frequency changes the outcome

How often interest compounds — annually, quarterly, or monthly — affects your final return even at the same stated annual rate. More frequent compounding means interest gets added back to your balance sooner, so it starts earning its own interest earlier. Monthly compounding will always produce a slightly higher return than annual compounding at an identical stated rate, which is why the compounding frequency listed on a financial product is worth checking, not just the headline interest rate.

Where each one shows up in real life

Most Indian bank loans — home loans, car loans, personal loans — actually use a reducing-balance method (closely related to compound interest) rather than simple interest, which is why EMI calculations use a different formula entirely. Fixed deposits and recurring deposits in India typically use compound interest, often compounded quarterly. Simple interest shows up more in specific short-term lending products, certain informal loans, and some educational contexts where the math is deliberately kept straightforward.

The practical takeaway

Over short periods, the difference between simple and compound interest is often small enough not to matter much. Over years, particularly for investments and long-term deposits, that gap compounds — quite literally — into a meaningfully different outcome. When comparing two financial products, always check whether they're quoting simple or compound interest, and if compound, at what frequency, since these details change the real return far more than they might first suggest.
